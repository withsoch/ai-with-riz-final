Most advice on shadow AI in the workplace starts with a policy, a ban or a list of approved tools. That treats it as a discipline problem: people are breaking rules, so tighten the rules.

I ran operations for ten years at Careem, Bolt and Wise, and the pattern was the same every time someone worked around a system. The workaround was telling me something. Shadow AI is the newest version of that signal.

So my position is this: every instance of unapproved AI use marks a process that is slow, unwritten or badly designed. Read it as a map of what to fix, and build the approved version properly.

## What is shadow AI in the workplace?

Shadow AI is the use of generative AI tools, such as public chatbots, for work tasks without the IT department's knowledge or governance. It is the same idea as [unsanctioned AI use described by TechTarget](https://www.techtarget.com/ai/tip/How-organizations-should-handle-AI-in-the-workplace): tools in daily use that nobody in charge has seen, approved or documented.

A plain example: an employee pastes a spreadsheet of customer data into the free version of ChatGPT to summarize it, and the company never finds out. It descends from shadow IT, like staff using personal phones for business.

## Why do employees use AI tools nobody approved?

Because the task is slow and the approval queue is slower. Nobody wakes up wanting to defy IT. They have a report to write, a backlog of replies, a messy handoff, and a tool that fixes it in two minutes.

![Woman at a desk with paperwork and an open laptop, working through a task](/blog/shadow-ai-workplace-process-problem-inline-1.jpg)

*The shortcut appears where the work is slow, and that is exactly where the process needs attention.*

The numbers point the same way. [Forbes reports](https://www.forbes.com/sites/bryanrobinson/2024/08/02/shadow-ai-the-controversial-2024-trend-that-could-create-disaster-experts-say/) that 45% of executives have adopted a wait and watch approach while 75% of employees are already using AI at work. Genpact's Sreekanth Menon attributes unapproved use to missing policies and slow IT approval. A separate 2024 survey found over 60% of companies lack a formal AI policy.

That is a gap in leadership and process, not in character. Where the work is written down and fast, people have little reason to improvise. Where it lives in someone's head, they will find a shortcut.

## What does shadow AI actually put at risk?

The mechanism is simple. Pasted data typically leaves the device, is processed on the provider's servers, and depending on the tool and account settings may be stored, used to improve models or kept in logs.

Once that happens, it is outside your control. The core risks are:

- **Data privacy violations.** Uploading personal or health information to a public model.
- **Confidentiality breaches.** Intellectual property or client information leaving the company.
- **No audit trail.** Untracked model use makes it hard to explain how a result was produced.

These are real, and I am not dismissing them. But the risk scales with how much sensitive data your processes push through unofficial channels, which is a process question before it is a security one.

## Why does banning shadow AI make it worse?

A ban does not remove the slow process that caused the behavior. It only removes your view of it.

![Person holding a smartphone beside a desk in an office, partly hidden from view](/blog/shadow-ai-workplace-process-problem-inline-2.jpg)

*A ban moves the usage onto personal phones, and you lose sight of where the work hurts.*

People keep using the tools, now on personal accounts and personal phones, and stop mentioning it. You lose the one thing you had: a chance to see where the work hurts.

There is a second problem. Automation amplifies whatever thinking is already there. A muddled, undocumented process run through hidden AI produces muddled output faster, with no one checking it. That is worse than either problem alone.

**A ban trades a visible problem for an invisible one.**

## How do you turn shadow AI into a system that works?

Work in this order. The sequence matters more than any tool.

1. **Ask what people use and why.** Make it safe to answer. Amnesty first, or you will get silence. Note the task each tool serves, not just the tool.
2. **Write down the process behind each use.** What triggers it, what goes in, what a good result looks like, who checks it. Half the time, writing it down shows the process needs fixing before any AI touches it.
3. **Set a one-page rule on data.** A short AI usage policy: what never gets pasted (customer personal data, client confidential material, anything regulated), and who to ask when unsure. One page gets read. Twelve do not.
4. **Ship an approved version of the best use cases.** Pick the two or three that are frequent and clearly valuable. Build them inside your own systems, with the data rules built in. A shipped, imperfect version beats a perfect one still in review.

## FAQ

### Can you give me an example of shadow AI?

An employee pastes a customer spreadsheet into the free version of ChatGPT to summarize it, without telling anyone. Another common one: a team member drafts client emails with a personal AI account because the approved process for templates is slow or does not exist.

### Is shadow AI a security risk?

Yes. Pasted data leaves your control and, depending on tool and account settings, may be stored, logged or used to improve models. The main risks are privacy violations, client confidentiality breaches and untracked usage that hurts auditability.

### Does banning AI tools at work stop shadow AI?

Rarely. The slow or unwritten process that drove the usage is still there, so people keep going, now on personal accounts and out of sight. A ban removes visibility, which is the one thing you need to fix the underlying problem.
